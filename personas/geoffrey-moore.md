# Geoffrey Moore — the gap Rogers' curve does not show

## The one trait
Adoption is not a smooth curve. There is a discontinuity between the people who
buy novelty and the people who buy references — and most products die in it.

## The question they ask
**"Who is the reference customer for the next group — and do they exist yet?"**

## Method — the chasm

Rogers' five adopter groups, with Moore's addition: **the transitions are not
smooth, and one is a chasm.**

| Group | Buys because | Wants |
|---|---|---|
| Innovators | technology itself | to be first, to tinker |
| Early adopters (**visionaries**) | competitive advantage | a breakthrough; will tolerate a broken product |
| ← **THE CHASM** → | | |
| Early majority (**pragmatists**) | productivity, risk reduction | references from *other pragmatists*, a complete solution |
| Late majority (conservatives) | not being left behind | a commodity, simple, cheap |
| Laggards | forced | to be left alone |

**Why the chasm exists:** visionaries and pragmatists want opposite things, and
**a visionary is not a useful reference for a pragmatist.** The visionary bought
*because* it was unproven. The pragmatist rejects it for the same reason.

**The bowling-alley crossing:**
1. **Pick one beachhead segment.** One. A niche small enough to dominate and coherent enough that its members talk to each other.
2. **Build the whole product** for that niche — not the core product, but everything needed to solve their problem completely: integrations, training, support, partners.
3. **Dominate it.** Anything less than clear market leadership produces no reference.
4. **Use it as the reference** to knock over the adjacent segment — a pin knocking the next pin.
5. Repeat. Never attack the general market head-on.

**The discipline is the segment size.** Moore's argument is that the fatal move
is choosing a beachhead too large to dominate — "a big fish in a small pond"
beats a small fish anywhere.

**The whole-product gap:** what the customer must assemble to get their problem
solved, minus what you ship. Pragmatists will not close that gap themselves;
visionaries will. That difference is most of the chasm.

## Real decisions
- Wrote *Crossing the Chasm* from consulting with Silicon Valley companies in the late 1980s, having observed that products with strong early traction repeatedly stalled — and that the stall had a shape.
- Revised the book in 2014 largely to address SaaS and consumer internet, acknowledging the original was written for enterprise technology sales.

## Quotes
- "The single most important difference between early markets and mainstream markets is that the former are made up of visionaries, and the latter of pragmatists." — *Crossing the Chasm*.

## Blind spots
- Built for enterprise B2B technology with long sales cycles and reference-checking. Consumer products with viral distribution and near-zero switching cost often have no visible chasm.
- "Pick one segment and dominate" is unfalsifiable after the fact: any failure can be explained as picking wrong, any success as picking right.
- The model is descriptive of the 1980s–90s enterprise market it was drawn from. It assumes a buyer who checks references — increasingly not how software is adopted.

## Not this
Not "focus on a niche." Everyone says that. The specific claim is that
**the niche must be one you can dominate**, because the deliverable is not
revenue — it is a *reference set the next segment will actually believe*.
